## The broad problem

Large game projects often inherit systems from the work that came before them. That can save time, but it can also carry assumptions that no longer fit the new project.

For Assassin’s Creed Valhalla, I worked in a small group with one engineer and one animator. The three of us built the system specifically for the project instead of copying and adjusting the previous solution.

## Why rebuilding mattered

One thing that sticks out is the size of the group compared with the scope of the problem. It meant that animation, code, and technical implementation could not be treated as separate hand-offs. We needed to work through the system together and make decisions with the actual project in front of us.

Rebuilding also forced us to make the logic explicit. Instead of preserving behavior because it already existed, we could ask what Valhalla needed, which parts were still useful, and which assumptions should be removed.

## What the experience taught me

I like this example because it represents how I approach technical animation. I do not see the job as connecting assets after the important decisions have already been made. The technical animator can help shape the system itself, especially when the visible result depends on how animation and gameplay logic meet.

The implementation details are intentionally limited here, but the team structure and approach are the important part: one engineer, one animator, and one technical animator, building a solution around the needs of the project.

> Starting from scratch was not the goal by itself. The goal was to make the assumptions fit the game we were actually building.
